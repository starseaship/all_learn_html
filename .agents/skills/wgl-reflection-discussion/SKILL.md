# WGL Reflection & Discussion Writing

Use this skill for Zoe's WGL / Hidden Figures reflection assignments, group-discussion reflections, and required replies to classmates.

## 1. Reflection: fixed 2 × 11 sentence format
When the assignment says **Reflection (2 × 11 sentences)**, write exactly two 11-sentence paragraphs.

Each paragraph must follow:

- Topic sentence: **1 sentence**
- Point 1: **3 sentences**
- Point 2: **3 sentences**
- Point 3: **3 sentences**
- Concluding sentence: **1 sentence**

This is the fixed **1–3–3–3–1** structure.

### Formatting rule
Insert a blank line after every 1 / 3 / 3 / 3 / 1 block so the structure is visually obvious:

Topic sentence.

Point 1 sentence 1. Point 1 sentence 2. Point 1 sentence 3.

Point 2 sentence 1. Point 2 sentence 2. Point 2 sentence 3.

Point 3 sentence 1. Point 3 sentence 2. Point 3 sentence 3.

Concluding sentence.

Do this separately for both 11-sentence paragraphs.

## 2. Paragraph 1: class content
Paragraph 1 should explain what happened in class and what was discussed or understood.

- Prefer **“In class, we discussed…”** when the lesson centered on discussion.
- Do not default to “today’s class” when “in class” is sufficient.
- Prefer **discussed / talked about / examined / considered** over repeatedly saying “we learned.”
- Choose three clearly different class points.
- Each point needs three complete sentences, ending with periods.
- Include concrete book events, teacher questions, page references, or discussion examples when available.
- Do not invent activities or details that were not mentioned.

## 3. Paragraph 2: thoughts and reflection
Paragraph 2 should focus on Zoe's thinking, not repeat Paragraph 1.

Each 3-sentence point should normally develop:
1. the specific thing that caught her attention;
2. why it mattered / what she thought about it;
3. how it changed, deepened, or complicated her understanding.

Good reflection topics include:
- unfair treatment or workplace systems;
- professional confidence and checking evidence;
- role models and women in leadership;
- how a character's environment affected career opportunities;
- a title's double meaning;
- a teacher's question that changed how the event was understood.

Avoid vague statements such as:
- “It was interesting.”
- “I learned a lot.”
- “It was good.”

Replace them with the exact event, why it mattered, and what changed in the student's thinking.

## 4. Use teacher questions as evidence
If the teacher asks a specific question, use it as one of the reflection points when relevant.

Example pattern:
- The teacher asked why the data/result looked wrong.
- The class checked the process and found the actual cause.
- The reflection explains what this shows about assumptions, evidence, checking inputs, or professional judgment.

Keep facts consistent with the book. If page numbers are supplied, preserve them accurately.

## 5. English level and tone
- Use clear, natural academic English suitable for a university class.
- Keep vocabulary accessible; avoid unnecessarily ornate wording.
- Prefer straightforward sentences over long, heavily nested structures.
- Make the reflection sound like a real student response rather than a formal research paper.
- Preserve the user's own viewpoint when she provides it.

## 6. Chinese translation
When the user asks for Chinese:
- translate the complete final version;
- preserve the exact 1–3–3–3–1 blank-line structure;
- keep meaning natural rather than mechanically literal;
- do not omit any sentence or point.

## 7. Group discussion reflection
When the prompt asks “What did you learn through your group and/or class discussions?”:
- use specific examples from the actual discussion;
- explain what happened, what was learned, and how the student's thinking developed;
- focus on learning rather than listing activities;
- use contrasts such as **Before the discussion… / After hearing…** only when they genuinely fit;
- do not invent what group members said.

## 8. Reply to a classmate
When a thoughtful reply is required:
- respond to at least one **specific idea** in the classmate's post;
- briefly explain why that idea stood out;
- add one new thought, connection, example, or question;
- do not write only “I agree,” “Good point,” or generic praise;
- normally keep the reply concise (about 3–5 sentences) unless the teacher specifies another length;
- keep the tone polite, natural, and student-like.

A useful pattern is:
1. identify the classmate's specific point;
2. explain your reaction or connection;
3. add a related idea from the chapter/discussion;
4. optionally end with a thoughtful observation or question.

## 9. Accuracy
- Treat uploaded book pages and teacher instructions as the primary source.
- Do not invent page content, group comments, teacher questions, or historical facts.
- When outside facts are needed, verify them before using them.
- If the task is only writing/reflection, do not add unnecessary research.
